Your Caffeine Blueprint

Stop guessing when to stop - find your own window so caffeine works for your focus, not against it

The Science (30 Seconds)

Caffeine takes about 5 hours to drop by half. Half of a 2 PM coffee is still in you at 7 PM, a quarter of it at midnight. But 5 hours is only the average - some people clear it five or six times faster than others. Same cup, same hour: one person is clean by evening, the next carries a third of it into bed. That is why the calculator below gives you a window, not a line.

You can fall asleep fine and still lose the deep part of the night. A typical adult night holds only 80-100 minutes of deep sleep. An ordinary late coffee costs around 11 minutes of it - about 12%. A 400 mg dose - four coffees, or one scoop of strong pre-workout - costs around 30 minutes, about 33% of the night's supply. For scale: people over 60 usually get under 20 minutes of deep sleep a night in total.

How much matters as much as when, and "a cup of coffee" is not an amount: the same 8 oz of brewed coffee runs anywhere from 95 to 200 mg. The bigger the dose, the longer your body needs - so the calculator works from what you are actually drinking.

What losing deep sleep actually costs - and why you won't feel it

Researchers took nine healthy young adults and used quiet sounds to nudge them out of deep sleep and into light sleep. Nobody was woken. Not a single minute came off their total sleep or their dreaming sleep. Same hours in bed, same hours asleep. Just shallower. After three nights, their bodies had become about 25% worse at handling sugar - down to the level found in people at high risk of type 2 diabetes. That is roughly what you would get from carrying an extra 18-29 lb (8-13 kg). The calm-down side of their nervous system dropped about 15% - that is the part your ring reads when it scores your recovery. The balance tipped roughly 14% toward the stressed side.

One more thing worth knowing about daily use. After weeks of it, most of what your morning cup does is bring you back to normal rather than lift you above it. You are not getting a boost. You are climbing out of the hole the last one dug.

The more deep sleep they lost, the bigger the hit. It scaled. That is why smaller losses still matter - there is no free amount you get to give away.

And that is exactly why it goes unnoticed. Nothing about those nights felt bad - same hours in bed, same hours asleep, nobody woken. What moved, moved in the blood work and in the heart rate, and neither of those has a feeling attached to it. "I feel fine" isn't a lie, and it isn't denial. It's just not the instrument that reads this one.

Tasali et al., 2008 - Slow-wave sleep and the risk of type 2 diabetes in humans (PNAS)

  • Time it, don't quit it. Push your caffeine into the morning - that is the only way a big dose has time to clear. After your window closes, decaf or herbal tea.

The Recovery Protocol

5 changes to your bedroom that let your brain actually recover - plus a maintenance tracker so it stays that way

1Bed
Buy In This Order

Do this: base, then mattress, then pillow, then bedding.

The order matters: how high your pillow needs to be depends on how far your shoulder sinks into the mattress. Pick the pillow first and you will be picking it twice.

The Foundation

A bed frame with flexible wooden slats outperforms solid boards. The slats flex with your body and extend the life of your mattress. Minimum size for two people: 63x79 in (160x200 cm).

Your Mattress

The top comfort layer should follow your contours. Pick firmness by your weight:

  • Under 130 lb (60 kg) - soft
  • 130-200 lb (60-90 kg) - medium
  • Over 200 lb (90 kg) - firm
  • Try before you buy - lie down in the store for 10-15 minutes
Your Pillow

Height depends on how you sleep:

  • Side sleeper - 4-5.5 in (10-14 cm), fills the gap between shoulder and head
  • Back sleeper - 3-4 in (8-10 cm)
  • Stomach sleeper - 2.5-3 in (6-8 cm), or no pillow at all
  • Fold test - fold it in half; if it stays folded, it's dead, replace it
Blanket and Bedding

Match your blanket to the season and room temperature. If you sleep with a partner who runs hot or cold differently, use two separate blankets. Natural fabrics only: cotton, linen, bamboo. Synthetics trap heat and moisture.

2Air
Temperature

Target: 65-75 F (18-24 C). The two directions are not equal - with a blanket over you, it is heat that costs you deep sleep and dreaming sleep. Cool barely registers. Cold is not free either: to fall asleep, your body dumps heat through your hands and feet, so a room cold enough to leave your feet cold shuts down the very route it is using.

Do this: aim cool, not cold. If you like it cool, wear socks. Give any new setting a week before you judge it.

Why the range is that wide, and why heat is the one to worry about

The largest in-home study of this covered about 11,000 nights of wearable and sensor data. Sleep quality was best between 68 and 77 F (20-25 C). Going from 77 F to 86 F (25-30 C) cost 5-10% of sleep efficiency - the share of time in bed you actually spend asleep. The same study found the best temperature differs noticeably from person to person, which is why this is a range and not a number.

On the cold side, study after study finds sleep stages barely move. No significant difference anywhere from 55 F to 73 F (13-23 C). In older sleepers, none between 48 F and 68 F (9-20 C). What makes the cold side safe is your blanket. That is why the bed comes first in this guide.

The socks part is not folklore. One thing predicts how fast you fall asleep better than anything else: the temperature gap between your hands and feet and the rest of your body. It beats your body temperature, your heart rate, your melatonin, and how sleepy you feel.

Okamoto-Mizuno & Mizuno, 2012 - Effects of thermal environment on sleep and circadian rhythm (J Physiol Anthropol 31:14)

Baniassadi et al., 2023 - Nighttime ambient temperature and sleep in community-dwelling older adults (Sci Total Environ)

Humidity

Target: 40-50% - narrower than most advice gives, because it is squeezed from both sides. Above 50%, dust mites can live and breed in your mattress. Below 30-40%, your nose and throat dry out.

Do this: winter, a humidifier. Summer or a damp home, a dehumidifier or the AC on dry mode. Don't dry laundry in the bedroom. One $30-60 CO2 meter reads all three numbers in this section.

Why 40-50%, and why summer nights are worse than the thermometer says

The upper limit is set by dust mites. They never drink - they pull water straight out of the air, and they need humidity above roughly 50% to keep a colony going. That one fact is why standard allergy advice is to keep indoor humidity under 50%. It holds them down, it does not wipe them out - but it is the difference between a mattress they can live in and one they can't.

The lower limit is your nose and throat. Below 30-40%, complaints of dry, stuffy air and sore eyes, nose and throat rise sharply in the research, and 40% tests better than under 30%. In winter, a heated home routinely sits below 30%.

And humidity is not a separate problem from heat. It multiplies heat. A room at 90 F (32 C) and 80% humidity produced more wakefulness and less deep sleep than 79 F (26 C) at 60%. Sweat that can't evaporate can't cool you, so if your summer nights are bad, humidity is likely doing more of the damage than the temperature reading suggests.

Arlian et al., 1999 - Reducing relative humidity to control the house dust mite (J Allergy Clin Immunol)

Wolkoff, 2018 - Indoor air humidity, air quality, and health: an overview (Int J Hyg Environ Health)

Ventilation

Target: under 1,000 ppm of CO2 overnight, ideally under 800. Ppm just means how much CO2 is in the air. Outdoor air sits around 400, so anything above that in your bedroom is air you have already breathed. This is the one number in this guide you can't feel. A bedroom slept in with the window shut averages 2,400-2,600 ppm by morning. More than double the limit, for seven or eight hours straight.

Do this: air the room out before bed. Then, if noise, cold and safety allow, leave a window cracked or the bedroom door open all night. If they don't, a ventilation unit or a trickle vent does the same job.

The full scale, and what ventilating actually changed

Outdoor air sits around 400 ppm. A well-aired room holds 600-800. A closed room with someone breathing in it drifts past 1,500. The 2,400-2,600 ppm figure comes from real bedrooms with one person sleeping in them, measured with the window shut.

When the same rooms were ventilated, measured sleep quality improved - and so did next-day sleepiness, ability to concentrate, and performance on a test of logical thinking. In one of the two experiments the entire intervention was an open window.

Strom-Tejsen et al., 2016 - The effects of bedroom air quality on sleep and next-day performance (Indoor Air 26:5)

3Light
At Night - Total Darkness

"Dark enough" is much darker than it feels. Ordinary room light - the level you would call almost dark - cuts your melatonin by 88%, and shifts your body clock more than half as much as full daylight does. About 1% of the brightness buys half the damage.

And it is not only about melatonin. One night under that same room light raised people's heart rate all night, cut their deep sleep and dreaming sleep, and left them worse at handling sugar the next morning. Light does not need to wake you, or even to look bright, to take the repair part of your night.

Do this: blackout curtains (95%+), tape over LED standby dots, chargers and routers out of sight. No curtains? A proper sleep mask.

Then test it: stand in the room at night with everything off and let your eyes adjust for a minute. Whatever you can still see is what you sleep in.

The numbers, and why this isn't just about melatonin

The 9,000 lux in that comparison is daylight - stepping outside on a bright day, or sitting right in front of a 10,000-lux therapy lamp. Here is what the rest of the scale looks like. Moonlight is under 1. A candlelit room is about 10. A normal living room in the evening is 100-200. An office is 300-500. Outdoors on a grey day is 1,000-2,000. Outdoors in the sun is 30,000 and up.

With that scale in hand, the research reads cleanly. At about 3 lux, melatonin dropped 11%. At about 106 lux - a dimly lit room - it dropped 88%, and the shift in the body clock reached 56% of the most that light can do. At about 9,100 lux, 98%. The authors put it plainly: half of the maximum shift from bright evening light can be had with just over 1% of that light.

116 people spent the eight hours before bed under room light - under 200 lux. It pushed the start of their melatonin later in 99% of them. It cut about 90 minutes off the stretch of time their bodies treated as night. During sleep itself, room light cut melatonin by more than half in 85% of trials.

Then the finding that changes what this section is about. Twenty healthy adults slept one night at 100 lux versus under 3 lux, eyes closed throughout. The lit group came out 15% worse at handling sugar the next morning. Their heart rate ran higher across the whole night. Their nervous system sat tipped toward alert rather than rest. They got less deep sleep and less dreaming sleep. And their melatonin came out no different from the dark night - closed eyelids block enough light for that. Which is the point worth taking away: light can cost you the repair part of the night without ever touching the hormone everyone talks about. Melatonin is the yardstick for evening and morning light, when your eyes are open. It is not the yardstick for the light you sleep in.

Honest limits: 20 people, one night, healthy young adults, laboratory conditions, and what was measured are early warning signs, not diagnoses. It is not evidence that a lamp gives you a disease. It is good evidence that "almost dark" is not a small thing.

Zeitzer et al., 2000 - Sensitivity of the human circadian pacemaker to nocturnal light (J Physiol 526)

Gooley et al., 2011 - Exposure to room light before bedtime suppresses melatonin onset (JCEM 96:3)

Mason et al., 2022 - Light exposure during sleep impairs cardiometabolic function (PNAS 119:12)

In the Evening - Warm Light Only

Colour is the second thing that matters - how much light there is comes first. Daylight bulbs at 5500K read as "noon" to your brain; warm bulbs run 1700-3200K.

Do this: after sunset, dim before you warm. Turn lamps off rather than turning them orange, and light the room from below eye level.

Screens - Worth Doing, Not the Fix

Do this: turn on Night Shift on iPhone/Mac, Night Light on Windows/Android, set to switch on at sunset. Thirty seconds, works forever, costs nothing.

Just don't expect it to solve screens. 167 people spent the hour before bed with the filter on, with it off, or with no phone at all. There was no difference between the three. The screen isn't only light. It's the scrolling, the deciding, and the being reachable.

What the Night Shift study actually found

167 adults aged 18-24. Seven nights in a row, each of them got the hour before bed in one of three ways: iPhone with Night Shift on, iPhone with Night Shift off, or no phone. Across the whole sample there were no significant differences in sleep outcomes between the groups.

Splitting by habitual sleep duration: among people averaging seven hours or more, the no-phone condition beat phone use - with or without the filter. Among people sleeping under six hours, Night Shift made no difference versus normal phone use either.

Keep the setting. It is free and the direction is right. Just don't let it be the thing you do instead of putting the phone down.

Jensen et al., 2021 - Does iPhone night shift mitigate negative effects of smartphone use on sleep outcomes (Sleep Health)

In the Morning - Go Outside, Not to the Window

Bright morning light is what sets your body clock, and indoors you simply can't get enough of it. Measured across ordinary days, people got about 1,000 lux indoors against 4,500 lux living outdoors.

Where the indoor-versus-outdoor numbers come from

Two more numbers for the ladder above. A dawn simulator alarm is worth more than nothing, and it sits below the therapy lamp. And across ordinary indoor and outdoor places, the typical reading was 1,175 lux outdoors against 179 indoors - eight times, before you even get a clear day. For the first two hours after waking, the camping study below measured 930 lux indoors against 3,070 outdoors.

Participants were measured for one week living normally, then one week camping with no electrical light. Average daily exposure during waking hours: 979 lux in the normal week versus 4,487 camping - more than fourfold. First two hours awake: 934 versus 3,074 lux. Between sunset and sleep the pattern reversed: 21 lux normally versus 8 lux camping.

After the week outdoors, every measure of their internal clock sat about two hours earlier. Their actual sleep and wake times had moved only about 1.2 hours. The clock moved more than the schedule did.

4Sound
The Target

Target: under 30 dB - quieter than most people picture. But loudness is not the main thing. What costs you the night is noise that starts and stops.

Your brain keeps listening while you sleep, and it reacts without waking you. In one study, road noise at only 45 dB kept people in brief stress reactions all night - heart rate and blood pressure spiking, nobody waking up. On paper the night looked normal. The body had spent it reacting.

Every scenario in that study averaged the same 45 dB. Only the ones that came in bursts did damage. A steady hum is not your problem. A door, a car, a notification, a partner shifting - that is.

The 45 dB study in full, and where a snoring partner sits on that scale

Twenty-six healthy young adults slept in a laboratory across four different transport-noise scenarios: road or rail, from steady to sharply broken up. All of them were set to the same 45 dB average level. The road noise that came in the sharpest bursts added up to more total time in those brief stress reactions. It also raised the next evening's cortisol. And it changed nothing about the overall shape of the night's sleep. Limits: 26 people, young, healthy, laboratory, and cortisol and stress reactions are body measurements - not a measurement of how tired you feel.

Now put a snoring partner on that scale. Mild snoring measures around 40-50 dB, moderate 50-60 dB. In a study of 1,139 people undergoing sleep testing, 12% exceeded 55 dBA for a tenth of the night. A partner who snores is not background nuisance. They are at or above the level that produced the effect above.

Thiesse et al., 2020 - Transportation noise impairs cardiovascular function without altering sleep (Environmental Research 182)

Wilson et al., 1999 - The snoring spectrum: acoustic assessment in 1,139 individuals (Chest)

What To Do, In Order
  • Kill the sudden noises you control. Phone out of the room or fully silent. No notifications. No ticking clock. Oil the door.
  • Then close the window. Worth 10-15 dB - but that trades air for quiet, which is why the real fix is getting your air from somewhere other than the window.
  • Then earplugs. Last, not first.
Earplugs

The best earplug is the one you don't pull out at 2 a.m. Wax and silicone sit flatter under a pillow and don't expand inside your ear, which is why people actually keep them in.

Foam blocks the most where it counts: low sounds, which is exactly snoring and traffic rumble. And the numbers above are laboratory ratings - what you get at home is well below them, because nobody puts them in perfectly at bedtime.

Do this: start with wax or silicone. If noise still gets through, move to foam.

If You Want Sound, Use Music - Not Noise

The research is specific about what kind. Across the trials the recipe is the same:

  • 60-80 bpm - a resting heart rate, so your pulse drifts down toward it
  • Instrumental - no lyrics, ever
  • Low volume - the studies ran 50-60 dB, a backdrop rather than a wall
  • Simple and repetitive - nothing to follow, nothing to anticipate
  • On a timer - 30 to 60 minutes, then off. In the trials this is listening at bedtime, not audio running until morning.

Yes: ambient, lo-fi, soft classical, new age, nature sounds, slow instrumental jazz. No: anything built on a hook and a chorus - that is stimulating music, and stimulating is exactly what it was written to be.

Why no lyrics, why no white noise, and where your own taste fits

Michael Jackson, Kesha, rap - all the opposite category. And soft classical was the most used category in the trials by far.

Lyrics are out for the same reason podcasts are. Words keep the language part of your brain working, and it keeps working after you are asleep. EEG recordings show the sleeping brain still sorting familiar voices from unfamiliar ones through every stage of the night.

The line is not the genre, it is the singing. Slow blues works instrumental, and stops working the moment someone sings.

One thing the research did favour: familiar music, and music from your own cultural background, beat unfamiliar music. So inside the recipe above, your own taste is an advantage rather than a compromise. If you want to check a playlist, here is what the trials deliberately avoided: lyrics, accented beats, percussion, syncopation.

If the audio comes from your phone, that is a step rather than a failure - it still beats scrolling. When you are ready to get the phone out of the room, a cheap speaker or a $5 clock radio carries the audio without carrying the phone.

And why there is no white noise machine in this guide. It is the standard recommendation everywhere, so its absence is deliberate. A systematic review of 38 studies rated the evidence for continuous noise improving sleep as very low. Their phrase, not ours: "which contradicts its widespread use." They also concluded it may harm sleep and hearing, and that better research is needed before anyone sells it as a sleep aid. There is a reasonable argument that steady sound masks sudden sound, and sudden sound is what the study above found harmful. But that is us joining the dots, not a result anyone has measured.

If you already sleep well with a fan or a sound app, nothing here says stop. It says we won't sell it to you as a fix, and if you are buying equipment, quiet at the source and earplugs have the better case.

Riedy et al., 2021 - Noise as a sleep aid: a systematic review (Sleep Medicine Reviews 55)

Jespersen et al. - Music for insomnia in adults (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews)

5Dust
The Lever That Actually Moves It

Dust mites trigger reactions that break up your night - congestion, sneezing, itchy eyes. Even without diagnosed allergies, less dust means better air and better sleep.

But your best weapon is not cleaning. Mites never drink - they take their water straight out of the air, and under 50% they can't keep a colony going.

Do this: hold the room under 50% humidity. Same target as the air section, and it beats cleaning.

What To Take Out
  • Out - carpets, open bookshelves, decorative pillows, stuffed animals
  • Plants are optional - the reason to move them is not CO2, which is nothing next to your own breathing. It is that permanently damp soil grows mould.
  • Keep only - bed, two nightstands, one closed wardrobe
  • Less stuff, less dusting - a near-empty bedroom is simply faster to keep clean

Your bedroom is for sleep and intimacy. Not work. Not storage. Not entertainment.

You came to fix the fog and the energy crashes. Most of what you have been told about "fixing your energy" is wrong - and the wrong moves quietly cost you the exact recovery your focus runs on. Here are ten of them.

The Zero Fog - Bonus

10 Recovery Mistakes

Ten things you do for energy that sound logical but work against your biology

1"You need exactly 8 hours."

Seven is the floor, not eight. Above that, your number is yours.

Eight is an average, not a target. But there is a floor, and it sits at seven: the expert panel that reviewed the evidence found that six hours or less is not enough for an adult. Don't round yours down to six because you're busy.

What about sleeping longer than eight?

The same panel deliberately set no upper limit at all. Some people run clean on seven, some need nine, and nine is fine.

If you regularly need ten or more and still wake up flattened, raise it with a doctor. Long sleep is more often a symptom of something than a cause.

Source: Watson et al., 2015 - AASM & Sleep Research Society joint consensus →

2"Sleep before midnight counts double."

Your body counts the hours you have been awake, not the hours on the clock. A steady schedule beats an early one.

Volunteers who lived a month with no clocks, on a 28-hour day, got the same deep sleep wherever their night happened to land.

How they proved it

The cleanest test of this ran for over a month. Volunteers lived with no clocks and no daylight, on a 28-hour day, so their sleep landed at every possible point of the body clock. Deep sleep never noticed. It tracked one thing only: how long they had been asleep.

Source: Dijk & Czeisler, 1995 - Journal of Neuroscience →

3"You can catch up on weekends."

Sleeping longer helps. Waking later hurts. Add the hours to the front of the night, not the back.

The half that fails is the half everyone does. Waking later drags your body clock later, so Monday starts out of step. Go to bed earlier; get up at your usual time.

What the lab found when it tested exactly this

In the lab, a weekend of unlimited recovery sleep did not protect the body. Across a repeating pattern of short weeks, people still ended up 9 to 27% worse at handling sugar.

A separate study split the two halves cleanly: people who slept longer on weekends tested better on attention, and people who woke later tested worse.

Source: Depner et al., 2019 - Current Biology →

Source: Zhang et al., 2020 - SLEEP (Oxford) →

4"A nightcap helps you sleep."

You sleep, but you don't recover. Two drinks are already enough to cost you that.

Here is the part that stings. Pooling 27 studies, falling asleep faster only showed up at five or more drinks. But dreaming sleep - the phase that handles memory and emotion - was already disrupted at two, and got worse with every drink after. So a one-glass nightcap costs you the recovery and doesn't even buy you the thing you drank it for.

Source: Gardiner et al., 2025 - Sleep Medicine Reviews, meta-analysis of 27 studies →
5"Sleeping pills fix insomnia."

Not one sleeping pill earned a strong recommendation. The only strong one in the guidelines goes to changing habits instead.

Look at what the people who write the guidelines actually concluded. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine has a guideline on drugs for chronic insomnia, and every single recommendation in it is graded weak. Not one drug earned a strong one. Meanwhile the same organisation's guideline on behaviour-based treatment gives its one and only strong recommendation to a structured behaviour-change program. Same organisation, same method, opposite verdicts.

Which ones they recommend against - and what about melatonin

Six are recommended against outright. Three of those six you can buy without a prescription: melatonin, valerian, and diphenhydramine - the antihistamine inside most over-the-counter sleep aids.

Melatonin does have a real use for jet lag and for shifting your clock across time zones. That is a different job from treating insomnia.

And nothing here is a reason to stop something a doctor prescribed you. That is a conversation with them.

Source: Sateia et al., 2017 - AASM pharmacologic treatment guideline →

Source: Edinger et al., 2021 - AASM behavioural treatment guideline →

6"I'm fine on 5 hours - I'm built different."

You feel fine. Your reaction time, your decisions and your memory disagree.

48 adults were held at 4, 6 or 8 hours in bed for 14 straight nights. The 8-hour group stayed flat. The 6-hour group drifted down to the impairment level of one full night without sleep; the 4-hour group reached two nights. And at the end, with their performance at its worst, those people reported feeling only slightly sleepy.

But what about real short sleepers?

They exist. They are also rare - far rarer than the number of people who believe they are one. The safe assumption is that you are not, until a fortnight of proper sleep proves otherwise.

Source: Van Dongen et al., 2003 (PubMed) →

7"Counting sheep works."

Boring your brain quiet doesn't work. Give it something interesting enough to crowd the worrying out.

It was tested on 41 people with insomnia. It did nothing. But the reason is the opposite of what everyone assumes: counting fails because it's boring. It takes up too little of your head, and worry walks straight back into the space left over. In the same study, the group handed a vivid, absorbing scene fell asleep faster than the controls.

How to actually do it tonight

Pick a place you know well and walk it in detail - the door, the light, what's underfoot, what you can hear.

Two rules for the place you pick. Detailed enough to occupy you completely. And neutral enough that there is nothing in it to solve - a work problem in vivid detail fills the same space, but brings the tension with it.

Source: Harvey & Payne, 2002 - Behaviour Research and Therapy →

8"Night owls are just lazy."

About half of it is inherited. Forcing a true owl onto a lark's schedule builds sleep debt, not discipline.

Across 8,753 pairs of twins, roughly half of whether you're a lark or an owl is inherited. Not a character flaw - and not a life sentence either, which is the useful half.

Source: Koskenvuo et al., 2007 - nationwide twin study, 8,753 pairs →
9"Snoring is harmless."

Loud, regular snoring can mean your airway is closing at night. It is common, it is very treatable - get it checked.

The medical name is sleep apnea: your airway briefly collapses through the night and your brain gets less oxygen each time. "Common" undersells it - an estimate published in the Lancet puts 936 million adults aged 30 to 69 worldwide in the mild-to-severe range. Get it looked at too if you wake up tired no matter how long you slept.

Why treating apnea may not be the end of it

Of those 936 million, 425 million sit at moderate-to-severe.

Treat it as a separate job from this guide. Fixing apnea fixes why you stop breathing at night. It doesn't tell your body when to sleep, and it doesn't switch your head off. Plenty of people get treated, sleep through the night, and are still tired - because those parts were never the airway's job.

Source: Benjafield et al., 2019 - Lancet Respiratory Medicine →

10"A white noise machine fixes a noisy bedroom."

Fix the noise at the source first, earplugs second. If you do want sound, the answer is music, not noise.

It is the most recommended sleep gadget there is, on some of the thinnest evidence there is. A review of 38 studies rated the evidence for continuous noise improving sleep as very low - in their words, "which contradicts its widespread use" - and flagged that it may harm sleep and hearing. What the research points at instead is bursts: noise that keeps starting and stopping drove stress responses at just 45 dB (a quiet room with a fridge humming), while the same level held steady did not.

The one place white noise earns its keep - and what "music" actually means

If a fan already works for you, keep it. Nothing here says stop. It says don't buy a machine expecting it to fix your nights.

And there is one place it does earn its keep - just not on sleep quality. Do you fall asleep to podcasts, videos or scrolling? Then swapping to sound without words is a real win. Not because the noise helps you sleep, but because you are dropping a habit that keeps your brain at work.

Music has the better case. A Cochrane review pooled 10 trials in 708 people with insomnia symptoms. Music improved how well they said they slept, on evidence rated moderate certainty - the strongest support any audio option has, and a different league from white noise.

But "music" there is a specific thing, not whatever you like. Across the trials the recipe is consistent: 60-80 bpm (roughly a resting heart rate), instrumental, low volume, simple repetitive structure, soft melodies. What they deliberately avoided: lyrics, accented beats, percussion, syncopation.

So ambient, lo-fi, soft classical, new age, nature sounds, slow instrumental jazz - yes. Michael Jackson, Kesha, rap, anything built on a hook and a chorus - no. That is the opposite category: stimulating music, which is exactly what it was designed to be. And lyrics are out for the same reason podcasts are - words keep the language part of your brain working, and it keeps working after you fall asleep.

Two side notes if you like to go digging. The "pink noise deepens sleep" studies people cite used sound pulses timed to brainwaves by laboratory equipment, not an app playing pink noise all night. And brown noise is the most hyped of the three, and the least studied.

Source: Riedy et al., Sleep Medicine Reviews →

Source: Jespersen et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews →
